Selected work

01 / 2026 · shipped to pilot

Project budget control, end to end

Discovery, market comparison, product decisions, spec, rollout

A site engineer raises a request, the request consumes project budget, the budget position decides which approval route it takes, and finance reports on what was spent and against what. On the legacy platform those four things lived in four places and agreed with each other only by accident. Each part worked. The seam between them was where the money went missing.

Discovery put numbers on it. Across eighteen months of production data, 31% of request lines were creating a brand-new material record, and 68% of those duplicated an item that already existed, so category spend was a guess rather than a figure. Interviews explained why: searching the catalogue took longer than typing the item name, so people typed it. The data problem and the usability problem were one problem seen from opposite ends.

I also looked at how three competing ERP products in this segment handle the same domain, through their documentation, demo environments and release notes. Every one of them treats an over-budget request as an exception to be blocked or overridden by a privileged user, and every one shows budget as a report on a separate screen. Neither is a technical limitation. Both are old decisions nobody revisited, which is where the opening was.

The finding that killed our first design: 17% of real requests were over budget. Blocking them, the obvious thing to do and what every competitor does, would have broken one in six real requests. Over-budget isn't an error state. It's a different approval route.

Budget is consumed at submit rather than at approval, because two requisitions each within the remaining balance can both pass approval and jointly exceed it, and nothing in the system would have caught it. The consumed amount and its currency are stored on the requisition, so a refund on rejection is exact even after the rate moves.

02 / platform infrastructure

An approval engine that hands data back

Problem framing, interface design, cross-module negotiation

Our approval engine could record that something had been approved. It could not carry anything produced during the approval, such as who the next approver should be or which status the record ought to land in. Every module was working around it, each in a different way.

I specified an outcome model. A step declares what it collects, the engine hands those values back, and the requesting module decides what they mean. Some values are fixed in the configuration and never shown to the approver at all, which is how a status transition became a setting rather than a release.

The same work surfaced a quieter problem. A permission check on an unrelated screen was silently letting some users skip approval altogether: the button fell back to a plain save instead of a submit, and nothing anywhere said so. It is now a test case, because a fix that only lives in the interface is not a fix.

03 / AI add-on

Natural-language record creation

Pilot scoping, safety design, stop criteria

The strategic bet on our platform is that a user describes what they need and the system fills the form. I ran the pilot on purchase requisitions, because that is where a wrong suggestion is cheapest: the assistant never writes to the database, a person always reviews and submits, and a bad draft costs ten seconds rather than a mis-posted financial record.

Three things we learned by running it, not by planning it

Material groups are stored in English while the users speak Turkish, so searching them by text failed at the language boundary. Handing over the full list and letting the model do the matching removed the failure entirely.

Row-level permissions filter silently, so the assistant once told someone their company had no requisitions when it simply could not see them. Empty results now carry an instruction to state the limitation rather than conclude absence.

And reasons beat prohibitions. "The user already sees the table" stopped it re-listing rows where "do not repeat the rows" never had.

Before the pilot ran I agreed a stopping rule with the CTO. Below 35% acceptance we say the value is in the tool layer rather than the chat surface, and we stop, instead of keeping a feature alive because it demos well.
04 / in production

Blue-collar timesheet, restructured

Legacy and new data under one approval model

Site supervisors were approving hours across two data models, one inherited and one new, reconciling them by eye. I restructured the module around a day-header approval: a supervisor approves a day rather than a scatter of rows, over a view that unifies legacy and new records so the seam is invisible to the person doing the work.

05 / in production

Transmittal management, end to end

Migration, print templates, follow-up, write-back

Document transmittals moving between the legacy platform and the new one: print templates, migration in deltas rather than one cut, sync triggers, and a write-back path so the two systems stop disagreeing with each other. The part that changed people's days was the follow-up screen. Before it existed, "where is this document" was a phone call.

How I work

  1. The brief is not the problem statement. "Rebuild purchase requests" was a sentence about scope. The problem was that people were bypassing a catalogue because it was slower than typing, and it took two weeks of data and nine interviews to be able to say that out loud.

  2. Every decision gets written down with the alternative we rejected. Not to look rigorous. So that when someone reopens it three sprints later, we're arguing about new information rather than re-running the old argument.

  3. Look outside before designing. For the budget work I went through three competing ERP products and asked our two closest customers what they had used before and why they left. The most useful thing it gave me was not a feature list. It was the confidence that a behaviour everyone shipped was a habit rather than a requirement.

  4. Every target metric gets a counter-metric. Making the catalogue mandatory would have improved data quality and wrecked entry speed. If I hadn't been measuring time to submit, that would have shipped as a success.

  5. Settle arguments with a model, not with seniority. Finance wanted budget consumed on approval, purchasing wanted it on submit, and two weeks of opinion-trading had settled nothing. I took two real requisitions from the legacy data, each individually within budget and jointly over it, and showed there was no point at which the approval-time model would have flagged either. That ended it in one meeting.

  6. Done means demonstrable. Ready means acceptance criteria written, test cases drafted, and no open question with a blocking owner. Done means it runs on the pilot environment and the events we said we'd measure are actually emitting.
